Motivation Forecast review Protocol audit Relevance

SANDLab uses computation to understand human physiology. Its heartbeat forecasting work raises a useful question: does a good heartbeat forecast preserve variation in annotated intervals? A prediction can be close in milliseconds yet retain little local variation. That variation may also contain annotation noise, so it needs a quality check before physiological interpretation.

I built a subject-level review of the lab's public benchmark, pairing point error with retained local variation. It identifies cases that deserve a closer look and checks the difference between requested input length and actually observed history—without treating a smooth point forecast as a failed predictive distribution.

How it works

The horizontal axis is interval RMSE in milliseconds. The vertical axis is predicted / observed local RR-RMSSD: a measure of successive-interval variation within 16-beat windows. A ratio of 1 retains the observed amount; below 1 retains less. This is not an autonomic-state measurement.

Analysis 2 — Requested history is not observed history

The saved setting says “8,192 beats,” but the evaluation starts near the beginning of each recording. The input wrappers extend shorter histories to the requested length. That distinction matters when designing a follow-up benchmark.

A controlled next comparison

  1. Inspect long intervals against the original ECG and beat annotations. Prespecify beat-quality checks, then report clean and flagged strata separately; do not silently remove difficult records.
  2. Use temporally separated forecast origins with the same genuinely observed RR history for every model. Keep participants as the unit of evaluation.
  3. Add persistence and appropriately sampled probabilistic forecasts. Measure point error separately from distributional calibration and the variation of generated trajectories.
  4. Only then test a prespecified physiological task. If calibrated trajectories retain useful variation, examine whether that helps the task; if not, revise the forecast target or representation.

Why this matters for the lab

For physiological modeling, a low point-error score is useful but incomplete. This review makes it easier to select diagnostic cases and specify a fair follow-up before asking whether forecasts support meaningful physiological inference. It extends the lab's reported variability analysis with a participant-level decision aid; it is not a new discovery that point forecasts can be smooth.

Methods note

This is a reanalysis of SANDLab's released benchmark summaries, not new model training or independent validation. The underlying MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm Database contains 18 long-term recordings from people found to have no significant arrhythmias; it does not establish performance in surgical or disease cohorts.

Definitions, source version, and limitations

Each participant's interval RMSE comes from the saved pooled result, converted from annotation samples to milliseconds at 128 Hz. The retained-RMSSD statistic is the median across 1,009 overlapping 16-beat positions of the source's window-averaged predicted/observed RMSSD ratio—not the mean of individual-window ratios or a standardized clinical HRV measurement.

The source averages 32 nearby origins × two repeated annotation-derived lead inputs. These are not 64 independent observations, and the overlapping positions are not independent replicates. No confidence intervals are constructed from them. The per-subject LSTM training protocol differs, so it is excluded from the matched foundation-model ranking.

Point-forecast smoothness does not establish poor predictive-distribution calibration. Long annotation intervals may include missed or noisy beats, with unverified cause; they can affect both forecast error and the variability denominator. The >2-second flag is descriptive QC, not a clinical cutoff. All 54 records remain in this comparison; the saved summaries cannot be cleaned retrospectively. Neither RMSE nor retained RMSSD diagnoses autonomic state, nociception, or disease. No restricted surgical data, raw forecast traces or clinical decision rule are used.
